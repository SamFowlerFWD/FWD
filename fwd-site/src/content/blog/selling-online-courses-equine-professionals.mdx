If you're a farrier, trainer, physio, saddle fitter or coach, you already sell what you know. You just sell it an hour at a time, in person, and there are only so many hours in a week.

Putting some of that into an online course or membership is the obvious next step. It's also easy to get wrong, usually by spending money in the wrong order.

I'm a farrier as well as a developer, and I've built two platforms in this space: [The Balanced Horse](/blog/building-the-balanced-horse), Rebecca Duke's training courses for horse owners, and [Hoofflix](/blog/building-hoofflix), the professional platform for farriers, where I'm now one of the directors. This is what I'd want someone to tell me before I started.

## Who's buying changes everything

There are two very different customers here.

**Horse owners** want a problem solved. Their horse won't stand for the farrier, or they want to understand what their trainer keeps saying about straightness. They'll pay for a clear course that helps, and they mostly watch on a phone.

**Professionals** want to stay current and be able to prove it. They care about CPD records, about things that help them with their own clients, and about whether the content is held to a professional standard.

Most of the decisions below depend on which of those you're selling to. Rebecca sells to owners. Hoofflix sells to professionals. They're built differently because of it.

## Subscription, one-off, or both

**One-off courses** are the easiest thing to sell. Pay once, keep it. People understand what they're getting, and you only need one good course to start. The downside is lumpy income: you mostly earn when you're launching or promoting.

**Subscriptions** give you steadier income, but you're signing up to keep feeding the library. People cancel when they stop getting something new, so a subscription with three courses in it and nothing planned isn't really a subscription.

**Both** is where established platforms tend to end up. The Balanced Horse runs monthly subscriptions for the course library alongside one-off purchases for standalone courses, plus discount codes for launches and seasonal offers. Hoofflix has subscriptions, individual course sales, team plans for practices and colleges, and free access for verified apprentices.

My usual advice: if you've got one strong course, sell it as a one-off first. Move to a subscription when you know you can add something worthwhile every month.

## Drip-feeding lessons

Drip-feeding means releasing lessons over time instead of all at once. On The Balanced Horse, a new subscriber gets the first few lessons straight away and more become available each week.

It does two useful things. It stops people watching a month's content in a weekend and cancelling. And it matches how training actually works. Nobody sorts out a horse in an evening, and a week of practice between lessons is worth more than the next video.

Don't drip everything, though. Reference material, and anything a professional might need in a hurry, should be there when they need it.

## Video that plays at the yard

Your audience isn't watching on a big screen with fast broadband. They're standing in a yard, phone in one hand, lead rope in the other, on one bar of 4G.

A platform that sends one big video file will buffer and give up. What you want is **adaptive streaming**. Each video is prepared at several qualities, and the player switches between them as the signal changes. Good Wi-Fi at home gets full HD. Patchy signal at the yard gets a lower quality that keeps playing. That's how The Balanced Horse is built, because that's how its students watch.

Two more things to check, on any platform:

- **Is the video protected?** On The Balanced Horse every video session is authenticated, so nobody watches without a valid subscription or purchase.
- **Can you upload from where you are?** Training videos are big files, and not every yard has decent broadband. Rebecca can upload large files reliably even on a slower connection.

Best test of all: open a demo course standing in your own yard and press play.

## CPD is the feature professionals pay for

If you're selling to professionals, a certificate of completion is nice. A CPD record that keeps itself is what makes them renew.

Farriers, for example, have CPD to keep up as part of their registration, and the [Worshipful Company of Farriers explains how the points work](https://wcf.org.uk/intro-to-cpd). Physios, saddle fitters and coaches will usually have a register or professional body asking for something similar. Right now, a lot of that evidence lives in a shoebox of certificates.

On Hoofflix, CPD records itself as members learn and produces the report a governing body wants. Assessment goes beyond multiple choice, because "watched a video" isn't the same as "understood it". And practices and colleges can buy team plans for their people.

Before you pick a platform for professional training, ask what it does beyond a completion certificate. If the answer is "not much", that's the gap your members will notice.

## What the big platforms actually cost

Here are the published monthly prices for three of the best-known course platforms, checked on their own pricing pages in November 2026. Two of them only show prices in US dollars.

| Platform | Entry plan | Middle plan | Worth knowing |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Teachable](https://www.teachable.com/pricing) | $39 a month, plus a 7.5% transaction fee | $89 a month, no transaction fee | Prices in US dollars |
| [Thinkific](https://www.thinkific.com/pricing/) | £92 a month, or £69 a month billed annually | £186 a month, or £139 a month billed annually | A branded mobile app is a $199 a month add-on |
| [Kajabi](https://kajabi.com/pricing) | $179 a month | $249 a month | Your own branded app needs the $499 a month Pro plan |

Card processing fees come on top on all of them. Teachable and Kajabi were both running Black Friday offers when I checked, so you might see something different for a few weeks.

To put that in context, the middle Thinkific plan works out at £1,668 a year billed annually, or £2,232 a year paid monthly. Over three years that's roughly £5,000 to £6,700, and at the end of it you've rented a platform rather than bought one.

That isn't a criticism. These are good products, and renting is the right call for a lot of people (more on that below). But it's the number to hold up against owning your own.

## What owning your platform costs

My prices are on [my pricing page](/pricing), and every project gets a fixed quote before anything starts. Custom apps start at £1,299 and most land between £2,000 and £5,000. A full course platform with subscriptions, one-off sales and apps on both stores is a bigger build, in the £5,000 and up bracket. Hosting starts at £20 a month, and I can spread projects over £2,000 into monthly payments.

On your own platform you'll also pay for video streaming, which grows as more people watch, and Stripe's card fees. What you won't pay is a revenue share, or a bigger plan every time you hit a limit.

Rebecca's platform costs her a fraction of what a Teachable or Thinkific subscription would, with no revenue share. She runs it herself day to day: courses, pricing, discount codes, and the reports showing where students finish a course and where they drop off.

## App store fees, and why many sell on the web

If you sell inside an iPhone or Android app, the store takes a cut. Apple charges 15% on in-app purchases for developers in its [Small Business Program](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/small-business-program/), which covers those making up to $1 million a year, and more at its standard rate. Google's [UK fee for subscriptions](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/112622) is 10% plus a 5% billing fee, and one-off purchases cost more.

On a £20 a month membership, 15% is £3 a month, every month, from every member who joined through the app.

That's why many course businesses take payment on their website, and the app is just where members log in and watch. In the UK there's a catch: Apple's [App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/) mostly stop you putting a "buy it on our website" button inside the app. So it needs designing with that in mind from day one. The Balanced Horse app is built that way, and it means Rebecca doesn't hand the App Store's cut over on her sales.

That may change. The UK competition regulator [consulted in 2026](https://www.gov.uk/cma-cases/apples-mobile-platform) on making Apple allow links to outside payment, but at the time of writing it hadn't made a final decision.

And you might not need a store app at all. A web app works on every phone, can sit on the home screen, and costs a lot less to build. For a first platform, that's often where I'd start.

## When an off-the-shelf platform is the right answer

Honestly? Quite often.

If this is your first course and you haven't got an audience yet, use Teachable, Thinkific, Kajabi or one of the others. Pay monthly, keep it simple, and find out whether people will buy. A custom platform won't sell your course for you. It makes things better and cheaper once people are already buying.

The signs you've outgrown a platform look more like this:

- Your monthly fee has crept up through the tiers and you're paying for features you don't use.
- You need things it doesn't do well: proper CPD records, team plans, free access for apprentices, or a mix of subscription and one-off that fits how you actually sell.
- You want your own branded app without the top-tier price.
- Members tell you the videos won't play at the yard.
- You want the relationship with your customers to be yours, not the platform's.

Whatever you use, own your domain name and your email list from day one. Those come with you if you move.

## What I'd do in your shoes

1. Make one course, on a problem people already ask you about.
2. Sell it on an off-the-shelf platform and see who buys.
3. If it sells, add a second, then think about a subscription.
4. When the platform starts costing more than it saves you, or can't do what your members need, that's the time to talk about your own.
