Most people who build software for the equestrian world have never stood on a yard at seven in the morning with a horse that doesn't want its feet picked up. You can tell.

The booking app wants you to type with both thumbs while one hand is holding a lead rope. The website looks lovely on office Wi-Fi and won't load in a field. The system thinks the customer and the patient are the same thing.

I'm a farrier as well as a developer. I've been building websites for farriers, saddlers and equine businesses since 2019, I run my own platform for [horsebox hire companies](/equestrian/horsebox-businesses), and I'm one of the directors of [Hoofflix](/blog/building-hoofflix), the professional platform for farriers. This is my attempt to write down what I've learned about how this industry works, what it needs, and who is actually at the end of the things we build.

If you run an equestrian business, some of this will sound obvious. That's the point. It's the obvious stuff that outsiders get wrong.

## Bigger Than People Think

The equestrian world gets treated as a niche. It isn't a small one. British Equestrian's [State of the Nation report](https://www.britishequestrian.org.uk/assets/EXTRA_Docs/Short%20State%20of%20the%20Nation.pdf) puts it at around 1.82 million regular riders in Great Britain and roughly 850,000 horses, with about 230,000 people working in the industry.

But the businesses looking after those horses are mostly small. Sole-trader farriers. Family-run yards. A saddle fitter with a van. A hire company with four horseboxes. Very few have anyone whose job is technology, and most have never had anything built for them. They've bought something off the shelf and bent their business to fit it.

That's the first thing to understand. A big market, made of small businesses, with hardly anyone building properly for them.

## Who Is Actually In It

### Owners and riders

The biggest group, and the one everybody else ultimately serves. Most are leisure riders keeping a horse alongside a full-time job. Sport England figures quoted in the same report say 88% of regular riders are women. They care enormously, they spend real money, and they have very little patience for bad software, because the horse is already taking up every spare hour they have.

### Yards and riding schools

Livery yards, riding schools, competition yards and equestrian centres. A yard owner is a landlord, a carer, a teacher and an events organiser at the same time, juggling horses, staff, arenas, owners and money, usually from a phone in a coat pocket.

### The professionals

Farriers, vets, equine dentists, physios, saddle fitters, nutritionists and instructors. Mostly mobile, mostly self-employed, usually booked weeks ahead. Several are regulated. Farriery is protected by law: under the [Farriers (Registration) Act 1975](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1975/35), it's an offence for anyone who isn't registered with the [Farriers Registration Council](https://www.farrier-reg.gov.uk/about/about-frc/) to shoe a horse, apart from a few narrow exceptions.

### Suppliers, makers and hire companies

Feed merchants, tack shops, farriery suppliers, horsebox builders, dealers and hire companies. They sell to everyone above, and they live or die on reputation.

### The learners

Apprentices, students, people retraining, and every qualified professional who has to keep learning and prove it. Often the people with the least money and the most need, which is why Hoofflix gives verified apprentices free access.

## What Makes This Industry Different

### The customer isn't the patient

In most businesses, the person paying is the person you're serving. Here, the customer is the owner and the subject is the horse. One horse might have an owner, a sharer, a yard manager and a groom, any of whom could book, pay or need to know what happened. Horses are sold and moved. Their history has to follow them.

The law already thinks this way. Every horse has a passport, and the [RCVS guidance on equine microchipping](https://www.rcvs.org.uk/veterinary-professionals/conduct-and-guidance/supporting-guidance/30-equines-and-microchips) sets out that owners must have their horses microchipped and keep details like a change of owner up to date with their Passport Issuing Organisation. If your software keeps everything against a customer account and nothing against the horse, it's modelling the wrong thing.

### The work happens outdoors

Horses live on yards, mostly in the countryside, often with poor signal. The person using your app might be wearing gloves, holding a horse, standing in the rain, or all three.

That means phone first, fast on a weak connection, big buttons and short flows. Anything a professional needs at the stable door, like a horse's notes or what was done last time, should work with no signal and catch up later. When I built [The Balanced Horse](/blog/building-the-balanced-horse), the video had to adjust itself to one bar of 4G at the yard, because that's where people watch it.

### It runs on cycles and seasons

Farriery runs on a cycle of a few weeks per horse. Teeth, saddles and vaccinations come round on their own schedules. A professional's diary is mostly rebooking, and a good system should do most of that for them.

Then there are the seasons. Competition and show season pushes horsebox hire into summer weekends, booked well in advance, with quiet weekdays in between. Winter brings mud, rugs, shorter days and different demand for lessons and arenas. Pricing, availability and reminders need to follow that rhythm instead of pretending every week is the same.

### Trust beats marketing

It's a small world. Everyone knows someone who knows you. Professionals get work by recommendation, yards by word of mouth, suppliers on reputation. Facebook groups and WhatsApp chats do more selling than any advert.

That changes what a website is for. Mostly it isn't there to persuade a stranger. It's there to confirm what a friend already said: that you're real, qualified, local and easy to book. Registration, qualifications, the area you cover, real photos of your work and a clear way to get in touch matter far more than clever copy. That's how I approach [equestrian website design](/equestrian/website-design).

### Everyone has read something online

Owners care, so they research. A lot of what they find is wrong, loud, or written by someone selling something. Professionals spend a surprising amount of every visit undoing what an owner read on Facebook.

Software can genuinely help with that. Hoofflix lets a farrier hand the owner an evidence-backed explanation after a visit, with the farrier's own name on it. The professional gets there before the search engine does.

### A huge range of comfort with technology

On one yard you might find a teenage apprentice who lives on their phone and a farrier who has been shoeing for fifty years and doesn't trust anything with a password. Both matter, and the tools have to work for both.

When I built Hoofflix's content tools for Dr Mark Caldwell, a self-confessed technophobe, the answer wasn't more training. It was making the system safe. Everything he creates lands as a draft, and nothing goes live until a person presses Publish. He can't break it, and once he knew that, he stopped being frightened of it.

### The money is real, but the margins are thin

People spend a lot on horses, but most equestrian businesses run on thin margins, seasonal cashflow and a lot of unpaid evening admin. Fees hurt more than they would elsewhere. A course platform taking a cut of every sale, a booking app charging per user, a card processor keeping its fee on a refunded deposit: it all adds up when your business is one van or four horseboxes.

## The People at the End

When I'm designing something, I picture a specific person using it. These are the ones I keep coming back to.

**The owner booking at ten at night.** They've finished work, fed the horse, and they're booking a lesson, a horsebox or a farrier visit from the sofa. They want to see what's free, what it costs and how to pay, in a couple of minutes. If they have to ring during office hours, a lot of them simply won't.

**The farrier at the stable door.** Hands full, signal patchy, three more horses before lunch. They need the horse's history in two taps, a quick way to note what they did, and the next visit booked before they leave. Everything else can wait for the evening, and ideally shouldn't need doing at all.

**The yard manager with twenty things on.** Liveries, lessons, staff, arena bookings and who still owes what. They don't want another app. They want one place that shows today and tells them what needs sorting. That's what a [booking system for a yard](/equestrian/booking-systems) should be.

**The hire customer who has never driven a horsebox.** Nervous, excited, maybe off to their first competition. They need clear instructions, a fair deposit and no surprises at collection. A hire company that makes that easy gets the repeat booking. I've written about [handling horsebox hire deposits and agreements](/blog/horsebox-hire-deposits-and-agreements) if that's your world.

**The trade buyer who knows exactly what they want.** A farrier ordering a particular shoe in a particular size, or someone comparing horsebox layouts and payloads. They want specs, stock and a quick way to order. Pretty pictures come second.

**The apprentice with no money.** Keen, skint and learning fast, almost always on a phone. Make learning easy for them and they'll be your customers for the next forty years. If you teach, have a look at [selling online courses as an equine professional](/blog/selling-online-courses-equine-professionals).

## What This Means If You Build for Horse People

These are the rules I hold myself to.

1. **Design for the yard, not the desk.** Phone first, quick on weak signal, big buttons, short flows. Test it outside.
2. **Model the horse.** Records, history and documents belong to the horse, and one account may have several horses or several people.
3. **Show your credentials.** Registration, qualifications, area covered, real names and real photos. That's how trust works here.
4. **Follow the cycle and the season.** Rebooking, reminders and pricing should match how the work actually runs.
5. **Keep it in one place.** Hoofflix started as two websites, a mailing tool, an events tool and a print account, none of which talked to each other. One login and one bill made more difference than any single feature.
6. **Make it safe.** Drafts before publishing, card holds before charges, and an undo wherever you can manage one. Especially for less confident users.
7. **Mind the fees.** Thin margins mean platform cuts and per-user pricing hurt more here than almost anywhere.
8. **Speak the language.** Nobody in this world wants to explain a shoeing cycle or a C1 licence to their web developer.

## Where That Leaves Equestrian Businesses

The good news is that the bar is low. In most corners of this industry, the competition has a slow website, a paper diary, a WhatsApp group and a spreadsheet. A business that makes booking easy, explains itself clearly and follows up properly stands out quickly.

You don't need a big system to start. For most sole traders, a fast website that turns up locally and a simple way to book or rebook will do more than anything else. For yards, hire companies and trainers, the bigger wins come from taking payments, deposits, documents and [courses](/equestrian/online-courses) off your hands. And if you're a [farrier or another equine professional](/equestrian/farriers-and-equine-professionals), the biggest win is usually getting the admin out of your evenings.
