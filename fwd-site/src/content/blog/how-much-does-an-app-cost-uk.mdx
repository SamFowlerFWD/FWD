A simple business app costs £1,300 to £5,000 to build in the UK in 2026. A full platform with customer accounts, subscriptions and apps on the App Store and Google Play costs £5,000 to £20,000 from a freelancer, and agencies typically charge £20,000 to £100,000 or more. Most small business apps then cost under £50 a month to run.

"How much does an app cost?" is a bit like asking how much a house costs. A one-bed flat and a farmhouse are both houses. But you can still give someone a useful answer, and that's what this guide does.

I build apps for businesses from Norwich, from small internal tools to platforms that live on the App Store and Google Play. Here's what they actually cost, what pushes the price up, and where you can save money without regretting it later.

## The Short Answer

Here's roughly what you'll be quoted in the UK in 2026:

| Option | Typical Cost | Best For |
|--------|-------------|----------|
| No-code app builders | A monthly subscription | Simple internal tools, testing an idea |
| Freelancer, focused app | £1,299-£5,000 | One job done really well, like bookings or a customer portal |
| Freelancer, full platform | £5,000-£20,000 | Multi-user platforms, subscriptions, iOS and Android apps |
| Agency | £20,000-£100,000+ | Large projects that need a team working at once |

That's the build. Running costs come on top, and they're covered further down because they catch people out. If you only need a website, my guide to [how much a website costs in the UK](/blog/how-much-does-a-website-cost-uk) is the one to read.

## What Actually Drives the Price

Two apps can look similar on screen and cost wildly different amounts. These are the things that make the difference:

**User accounts and roles.** An app where everyone sees the same thing is simple. An app where customers, staff and admins all see different things, and can only touch their own data, is a lot more work. It's also the bit you really don't want done cheaply.

**Payments.** Taking a one-off card payment is straightforward. Subscriptions, deposits held on a card, refunds and partial payments all add time.

**Integrations.** Every outside system the app talks to adds work. Accounting software, a CRM, a gate access system, the DVLA. Some have good APIs, some fight you every step of the way.

**Web, iOS, Android, or all three.** Each platform you target adds testing, store submissions and maintenance. More on this below, because it's where people overspend most often.

**The admin side.** Customers see the front of the app. You'll spend your time in the back of it, managing bookings, editing content, pulling reports. A good admin panel can be a third of the build.

**AI features.** Reading documents, sorting enquiries, searching by meaning rather than keywords. These are now genuinely affordable to add, but they need building carefully so they can't do anything daft.

## What You Get at Each Price Point

### £1,299-£2,500: A Focused Tool

One problem, solved properly. A quoting tool, a simple booking form with payments, an internal tracker that replaces a spreadsheet three people are fighting over. It runs in the browser on any device, and you can add it to your phone's home screen like an app.

**Good for:** testing an idea, or fixing one painful process before committing to anything bigger.

### £2,500-£5,000: Booking Systems and Customer Portals

This is where most small business apps land. Customer accounts, online payments, automatic emails and a proper admin panel.

[Fakenham Dog Field](/blog/building-fakenham-dog-field) is a good example. Customers book a slot, pay online, and the system sends them a gate code that only works during their booking. The owner doesn't need to be there to let anyone in.

**Good for:** businesses that run on bookings, jobs or orders and are drowning in phone calls and admin.

### £5,000-£20,000: Full Platforms

Multiple user types, subscriptions, native apps on both stores, and a lot going on behind the scenes.

[Hoofflix](/blog/building-hoofflix) is the professional platform for farriers: video, courses, CPD records and more than 8,600 research papers in one place, with apps on both stores. [Horsebox Hire](/blog/building-horsebox-hire-software) started as a booking system for one hire company and became a platform that several can run on, each with their own branding, vehicles and customers, plus AI document checks and DVLA licence checks.

**Good for:** businesses building a product, not just a tool.

## Work Out a Rough Cost in Three Questions

There's no app cost calculator that's worth trusting, because the details matter. But these three questions will put you in the right price band:

1. **Who uses it?** Just you and your staff is the cheapest. Customers logging in and seeing their own bookings, orders or records adds a lot. Several types of user, each seeing different things, adds the most.
2. **Does it take money?** No payments keeps it simple. One-off card payments add a little. Subscriptions, deposits, refunds and split payments add more.
3. **Where does it need to run?** In the browser on every device is the cheapest. Adding App Store and Google Play versions adds testing, store submissions and ongoing upkeep.

If your answers are "just us, no payments, the browser", you're probably looking at £1,300 to £2,500. "Customers, card payments, the browser" usually lands around £2,500 to £5,000. "Several user types, subscriptions, both app stores" is £5,000 and up.

## How Long Does It Take to Build an App?

A smaller app, like a quoting tool or a simple booking system, usually takes 3 to 5 weeks. Larger apps with several user types, payments and integrations typically take 6 to 12 weeks.

Store apps add time on top, because Apple and Google both review every app before it goes live, and they can ask for changes. The quickest route is nearly always to launch a smaller first version, see how people use it, and then add to it.

## Do You Actually Need an App on the App Store?

Often, no. And this is where a lot of money gets wasted.

A web app works on every phone, tablet and computer from day one. Customers can add it to their home screen, and it opens full screen like any other app. There's no store review, no waiting days for Apple to approve an update, and no store taking a cut.

A store app makes sense when you need things like push notifications on every device, people expect to find you by searching the App Store, or you're selling something where the "proper app" feel really matters.

If you do need store apps, building once and shipping to web, iOS and Android from a single codebase is far cheaper than building three separate apps.

One thing to plan for: Apple and Google take a cut of anything sold inside a store app, 15% for most small businesses and up to 30% otherwise. Plenty of apps take subscriptions on their website instead, so that cut never applies.

## The Running Costs Nobody Mentions

The build is a one-off. These aren't.

**Hosting and database: £0-£50/month for most small business apps.** Modern serverless hosting is cheap at small scale and grows with you. Be wary of anyone quoting £200 a month to host an app with fifty users.

**Store fees.** Apple charges a yearly developer fee of about £80. Google charges a one-off fee of about £20. You should own both accounts, in your business's name, not your developer's.

**Payment fees.** Stripe charges around 1.5% plus 20p for a standard UK card payment. Not a cost of the app as such, but budget for it.

**Text messages and email.** Booking confirmations by email cost next to nothing. SMS reminders cost a few pence each, which adds up if you send thousands.

**Maintenance.** Phones update, browsers change, Apple changes its rules. An app nobody looks after slowly breaks. My [hosting and maintenance plans](/services/hosting-maintenance) start at £20 a month and cover exactly this.

## How to Keep the Cost Down

**Start smaller than you think.** Build the one thing that solves your biggest problem, use it for a couple of months, then add to it. You'll spend less, and the second phase will be better because it's based on how people actually use it.

**Go web first.** Unless you have a clear reason to be on the stores, launch as a web app. You can add store apps later without starting again, if it's built properly.

**Don't pay for features "just in case".** Every feature costs money to build and money to maintain. If you can't say who'll use it and how often, leave it for later.

**Make sure you own the code.** If your developer disappears or you fall out, you need to be able to take the code somewhere else. Get it in writing before you start.
