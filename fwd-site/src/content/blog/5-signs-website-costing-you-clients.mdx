Most small business owners I speak to know, somewhere in the back of their mind, that their website isn't doing what it should. They just haven't pinned down exactly what the problem is.

Sometimes it's obvious: the phone hasn't rung from a website enquiry in months. Sometimes it's more subtle: a good conversation with a prospect goes quiet after they visit the site. Either way, the cause is often the same. The website is working against you rather than for you.

Here are five signs I see consistently. If more than two of these apply to you, your website is probably costing you real business.

## 1. It Takes More Than Three Seconds to Load

This is the big one, and it's measurable. Research consistently shows that bounce rates increase sharply after three seconds of load time. By five seconds, a significant proportion of visitors have already left. On mobile, it's worse.

The problem isn't just lost visitors. Google uses page speed as a ranking signal through its Core Web Vitals measurements. A slow site ranks lower in search results, which means fewer people find you in the first place. Slow load times compound: fewer visitors, lower conversion, and lower search visibility, all at once.

You can check your site's speed free at [Google PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/). Look at the mobile score especially. A score below 50 is a problem worth solving.

Template-built sites on platforms like Wix and Squarespace often struggle here because they carry code for features you aren't using. A site built specifically for your requirements loads only what it needs. The difference is often 2 to 3 seconds faster, which translates directly into fewer people leaving before they read anything.

## 2. It Doesn't Work Properly on Mobile

More than half of all web traffic now comes from mobile devices. For local searches, including "near me" searches, that proportion is even higher. If someone searches for your service on their phone and your site is hard to read, has text that requires pinching and zooming, or has buttons too small to tap accurately, they leave.

The frustrating thing is that most business owners check their websites on a desktop. The mobile version is a different experience, and if you haven't tested it properly, you may not know how bad it is.

Open your site on your phone and try to actually use it as a new customer would. Find your contact details. Read your pricing or services page. Try to make an enquiry. If any part of that feels awkward, it's costing you clients who are giving up rather than persisting.

Mobile optimisation isn't just about making things look smaller. It means rethinking layout, button sizes, text size, form design, and what information appears above the fold on a small screen. A proper mobile experience is designed for mobile from the start, not just a desktop site squeezed down.

## 3. The Design Doesn't Build Trust

A website is often the first real impression a prospect has of your business, and people form opinions about credibility within seconds of landing on a page. Design that looks dated, inconsistent, or cheap signals something about the business, even if that signal is unfair.

This isn't about having the most expensive-looking site on the internet. It's about a site that looks like a real business operating in 2026 rather than one that hasn't been touched since 2018. Outdated design patterns, poor-quality images, inconsistent fonts and colours, or a site that clearly uses a generic template tells visitors that this is not a business that invests in its presentation.

More specifically, trust signals matter enormously. These include visible contact details (not just a contact form with no address or phone number), testimonials or reviews, professional photography of the actual business and people involved, clear information about who you are and how long you've been operating, and any accreditations or qualifications relevant to your industry.

The businesses I work with often have excellent reputations built over years. But if the website doesn't communicate that trust, it's invisible to anyone who finds them online for the first time.

## 4. There's No Clear Call to Action

What do you want someone to do after they've read your homepage? If the answer is "ring me" or "email me", that action needs to be obvious, prominent, and repeated.

The most common problem I see is a website where all the services are described clearly, the work looks good, and then... nothing happens. There is no clear next step. No prominent phone number. No button that says "get a free quote". No invitation to do anything at all.

People are busy and easily distracted. Even if someone is genuinely interested in what you offer, if the next step isn't obvious they'll close the tab and tell themselves they'll come back to it. They rarely do.

Every page on your site should have one primary call to action. Not five options, not a vague "get in touch" buried in the footer: one clear, specific action you want the visitor to take. For most small service businesses that's a phone call, a quote request, or a booking. Make it prominent, make it simple, and make it appear more than once on the page.

## 5. You're Not Showing Up in Local Search

If someone in your area searches for what you do and you're not on the first page of results, you're effectively invisible to them. This is the most significant long-term problem because it means the website isn't generating the steady flow of new enquiries that it should.

There are several possible causes. The site might be slow (see sign one). The content might not clearly explain what you do and where you do it. The page structure might not match how your potential customers search. You might not have a Google Business Profile set up and optimised. Or the site might have technical issues that prevent search engines from reading it properly.

Google's AI Overviews, which now appear on a significant proportion of searches, have added another layer. These are the AI-generated summaries that appear above traditional results. Getting cited in an overview requires content that directly answers specific questions in a clear, structured way. Template sites rarely do this well.

If you have not looked at your search visibility recently, Google Search Console is a free tool that shows exactly which searches are finding your site and where you're ranking. The results are often illuminating.

## What to Do About It

If you're reading this and recognising your own site in two or more of these points, the question isn't whether to fix it. The question is what the most efficient path to fixing it looks like.

Sometimes that's a targeted set of changes: speed improvements, better mobile styling, a clearer CTA. Sometimes the underlying architecture of the site is the problem and incremental fixes have limited impact. A proper review tells you which situation you're in before you spend money.
