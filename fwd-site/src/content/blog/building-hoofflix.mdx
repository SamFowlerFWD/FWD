## I Was Brought In to Do Marketing

That was the job. Hoofflix had content, an audience, and a founder with genuine authority in his field — Mark Caldwell FWCF, a Fellowship farrier who has spent a career on the science of the equine foot and who literally wrote the book on it. What it didn't have was traction. So I was asked to look at the marketing.

I lasted about a fortnight before I had to go back and tell him the marketing wasn't the problem.

That's an awkward conversation to have two weeks into a paid engagement. You are, in effect, talking yourself out of the work you were hired for and asking for something much bigger instead. Most of the time it ends the relationship.

It didn't end this one, and I've thought a lot since about why.

## Why He Listened

I'm a farrier as well as a developer. That's an unusual combination and in this case it was the whole ballgame.

When Mark talked about medio-lateral balance or a broken-back hoof-pastern axis, I didn't need it explained. I'd stood under horses. I knew what a six-week cycle actually looks like, what a farrier can and can't do at the side of a stable with a rasp in one hand and a phone in the other, and why an owner asking "but I read online that..." is the most draining part of the week.

So the conversation we had wasn't the usual one, where a technical person explains what's possible and a subject expert tries to translate their life's work into requirements. We were just two people in the same trade, one of whom could also write software.

That's the foundation everything else got built on. Not a spec. A shared language.

## What I Actually Found

When I audited what existed, "Hoofflix" turned out to be four different things wearing one name:

- **hoofflix.com** — a WordPress site with 168 blog posts and 18 pages
- **A members' area** bolted onto that site, with around 143 paying subscribers on Stripe plans created years earlier and never touched since
- **scientifichorseshoeing.co.uk** — an entirely separate WordPress site running Divi, LearnDash (20 courses, 256 lessons), WooCommerce (175 products) and The Events Calendar (41 events)
- **A Vimeo library** of hundreds of hours of teaching video with no index anybody but Mark could navigate, plus Mailchimp for email and a print-on-demand account for the books

Four logins. Four bills. Four places for a customer to end up. A farrier who paid for the members' area couldn't see the courses. Someone who bought a course couldn't find the videos. Nobody's CPD was recorded anywhere at all.

You can't market your way out of that. No amount of clever copy fixes a product that makes people log in three times to get what they already paid for.

## The Bigger Realisation

Consolidating four platforms into one is a worthwhile job, but it's a *tidying* job. The more interesting thing was the gap underneath it.

Farriers, equine vets, physios, trimmers and saddle fitters all carry a professional obligation to keep learning and to evidence it. Most of them manage that with a shoebox of certificates. The general platforms — YouTube, Udemy, Teachable — were never built for them: no CPD records, no credentialing, no concept of a practitioner having clients.

But here's what actually mattered. The value isn't only in the teaching. It's in the ninety minutes a week a farrier spends doing everything *around* the teaching:

- Explaining to an owner, at the side of a stable, why the horse needs six weeks of a particular shoeing plan
- Measuring hoof balance by eye and hoping their memory of the last cycle is accurate
- Fielding a supplement claim an owner read on Facebook, with nothing to hand to answer it
- Writing up a case for a Fellowship submission with no decent reference library to cite from

None of that is education. All of it is the job. If a platform could make that part of the week easier, the education becomes the reason people stay rather than the reason they join. That's a fundamentally different product — not "pay to learn", but "pay to run a better practice".

So the brief changed. We weren't rebuilding a website. We were building the thing the industry should have had a decade ago.

## The Thing Nobody Warns You About

Here's what that actually asks of the other person.

Mark has spent a working lifetime on this. The research, the book, the teaching, the reputation — decades of it. And the proposal on the table was: give all of it to me, let me put it inside software you don't understand and can't inspect, and trust that what comes out the other side still has your name on it in the way you'd want.

That is not a technical decision. There's no spec document that makes it safe. It's a judgement about a person.

I don't think I fully appreciated at the start how much of the project was going to be that, rather than code. Every architectural conversation was really a trust conversation wearing a hat. Where does the video live? Who owns the Stripe account? What happens to all of this if you get hit by a bus? *(That last one is a real document in the repo, by the way — there's a business continuity section that starts "In the event of Sam Fowler's incapacitation". Writing it was one of the more useful things I did, and not for technical reasons.)*

The way you earn that is boringly simple. You tell people things they don't want to hear, early, while it still costs you something. The fortnight-in conversation about the marketing was the first instalment. There have been a lot since.

## The Laminitis Tool: The First Thing We Built Together

Before the platform rebuild was finished, we built something else entirely, and it turned out to be the proof the whole partnership needed.

Laminitis is the condition that keeps farriers and equine vets awake. Assessing it properly means taking calibrated radiographs and doing real geometry: rotation, sole depth, palmar angle, breakover. Mark had spent years developing the clinical model for it. What he didn't have was any way to give it to anybody other than by teaching it in person, one room at a time.

So we built it. He owned the maths — every measurement, every safety boundary, every clinical threshold, and he's still refining the engine now. I built everything around it.

What came out was a per-limb assessment tool covering all four feet, each holding a lateromedial radiograph, a dorsopalmar radiograph and a solar photograph. You place landmarks on the images with edge-snapping and a magnifier for precision, calibrate the scale from a physical marker of known length, and the engine derives seventy-plus measurements and a full shoeing prescription — including a compound palmar-angle model that activates automatically past five degrees, and a heel-elevation protocol capped by every safety boundary Mark insisted on. Reports generate as PDFs and share by expiring link.

Decades of clinical reasoning, in something a farrier can use on a tablet in a yard.

And then we did the obvious thing and made the same mistake the rest of the business had already made: we launched it on its own domain, with its own login, its own database and its own billing. A fifth island.

We caught it before it had real users, and folded the whole thing into Hoofflix — one login, one database, one bill, one deploy. Which is a slightly embarrassing story to tell in a post about platform fragmentation, and exactly why I'm telling it. It's an easy trap. We walked into it with our eyes open and still had to climb out.

## What Hoofflix Is Now

One platform. 347 pages, 713 API endpoints, 235 database tables. Next.js and TypeScript on Railway, PostgreSQL on Supabase, Stripe for money, Vimeo for video, Cloudflare R2 for everything else, and native iOS and Android apps from the same codebase via Capacitor.

That's the plumbing. Here's what it does.

### The Core: Four Ways to Learn

**Watch** — a Netflix-style streaming hub. Series, episodes, categories, featured rows, continue-watching, free preview episodes. It's the bit that gave the platform its name and it behaves the way people expect streaming to behave.

**Learn** — a full LMS. Courses split into modules and lessons, drip release so content unlocks on a schedule, timestamped notes bookmarked to the video timeline, chapter navigation, per-lesson Q&A, progress tracking, reviews and ratings, and a certificate at the end.

**Listen** — podcast shows and episodes with listen history and continue-listening, because a lot of this audience is in a van between yards.

**Read** — evidence-based scientific articles with categories, reading progress, and DOI and PubMed import so research papers arrive properly referenced rather than retyped.

### Assessment That Isn't Just Multiple Choice

Seven question types: multiple choice, true/false, multiple select, fill-in-the-blank, drag-to-order, matching, and image hotspot — which matters a lot when you're asking someone to identify the navicular bone on an actual radiograph.

Then nine interactive activity types on top: sequencing, matching, hotspot exploration, interactive video that pauses to ask questions, branching clinical scenarios with consequences, before-and-after sliders, multi-phase case-study walkthroughs, and practical submissions where an apprentice photographs their work and an instructor marks it against a rubric.

Quizzes sit at lesson, module or course level, draw randomly from a question bank, adapt their difficulty to previous scores, and ask for the learner's confidence on each answer — then show them a calibration chart afterwards, which is a genuinely uncomfortable and genuinely useful thing to see.

Around that: drag-and-drop anatomy labelling, an interactive 3D model of the equine distal limb with gait controls, and flashcard decks running SM-2 spaced repetition with a review queue that resurfaces the things you got wrong at increasing intervals.

### CPD, Done Properly

A design requirement from day one, not a bolt-on. Watch time and completed assessments automatically accrue CPD points against the categories the professional bodies actually use — practical skills, theory, business, health and safety, emerging tech. There's an annual goal, a running log, and a PDF report you can export and send to FRC, BFBA, AFA or WCF. Minimum watch percentages are enforced so the record means something.

Certificates are auto-generated with unique numbers and a public verification portal, so a client or an employer can check a credential is real.

### Keeping People Coming Back

Learning streaks with a weekly minimum, a twelve-week activity calendar, and four streak freezes a year so one bad week doesn't wipe out six months. Achievement badges. A community reputation system that promotes people from New Member to Expert on genuinely helpful contributions. Leaderboards, watchlists, colour-coded notes, and a recommendation engine.

## The Extras — The Bit That Actually Sells It

Everything above is a very good education platform. This next part is why a professional keeps paying for it.

**A reference library of more than 8,600 peer-reviewed papers**, ingested from sixteen-plus journals, AI-enriched with evidence levels, key findings and practical takeaways, and searchable semantically — by meaning, not just keywords — using pgvector embeddings. Plus a 12,000-entry evidence-linked glossary.

**An Evidence Checker.** Paste in a claim — anything an owner has read online — and get a verdict backed by the corpus: supported, partially supported, or contradicted, with the evidence quality rated.

**A Supplement Checker** doing the same for any equine supplement or ingredient, with dosage and safety notes.

**A Condition Explorer and Case Lookup** — enter the case, get the matched research, ranked by evidence level.

**A Client Handout Generator.** The farrier finishes a job, taps a condition, and produces an evidence-backed, branded information sheet for the owner — shareable by link, no account needed at the owner's end. Fifteen minutes of explaining, replaced by something the owner can take away and actually read.

**A Writing Studio.** A proper document editor for articles, case studies, research papers and theses, with cite-as-you-write pulling straight from the reference library, Harvard, APA and Vancouver formatting, Zotero import, auto-generated bibliographies and DOCX and PDF export. Built for the Fellowship candidate who currently does this in Word at midnight.

**The measurement suite**, which is where the laminitis tool ended up living, alongside:

- **Hoof Analysis** — per-horse case management with standardised photo capture, landmark annotation, computed hoof proportions, progression tracking across shoeing cycles, voice notes, and printable case reports with CSV export.
- **Physics for Horseshoers** — force, lever-arm and load-distribution calculators for everyday decisions.
- **HoofScan** — a free companion iOS app using the LiDAR sensor on a Pro iPhone to capture the foot in 3D, then orthographically projecting that mesh into the four standard views the measurement engine already understands. The scan doesn't replace the maths; it fills the photo slots automatically so the farrier goes straight to placing landmarks.

That suite lives at a URL we print as a QR code in the books. Someone reads a chapter, scans the page, and lands on the working tool. That's the whole strategy in one link.

**One-to-one case reviews.** Stuck on a case? Upload your photos, video and notes, then book a thirty-minute Zoom with Mark. Members get an automatic discount.

**A practitioner directory** — searchable, map-based, filtered by profession and location, with a paid "seeking work" badge. Pro Plus practitioners get an auto-generated website on their own domain, fed from the same profile data.

**And the commercial machinery around all of it**: subscription tiers from Free through to Enterprise, individual course purchases, a shop handling digital downloads, physical stock and print-on-demand books, gift subscriptions, discount codes, trade-association discounts, a tiered referral programme, webinars with Zoom integration and ticketing, an events calendar, synchronous watch parties, team and enterprise seats with aggregated CPD reporting for a veterinary practice or a college, and free verified access for farriery apprentices — because the people who need it most are the ones with no money.

## The Machinery Nobody Sees

154 admin pages. A full instructor dashboard with analytics, earnings and payout tracking. A marketing engine with campaigns, audiences, sequences, suppression lists and conversion attribution. Push notifications. 44 scheduled jobs handling drip emails, content publishing, journal ingestion, transcript processing, reference enrichment, research roundups and social posting. Six languages. Full audit logging, rate limiting, CSRF, CSP, brute-force protection, and GDPR export and deletion. Sentry on everything.

None of that is glamorous. All of it is the difference between a demo and a business.

## Teaching a Technophobe to Run Agentic Workflows

This is the part I'm proudest of, and it's got nothing to do with code.

All of the above is worthless without content, and the content lives in one head. Mark is nearly 70 and a self-confessed technophobe. He had no interest in learning a CMS, and — being honest about it — a certain amount of dread about the whole business. The realistic options were: he dictates, I type, and I'm the bottleneck forever; or I find another way.

So I gave him a direct line into the platform.

Hoofflix runs a Model Context Protocol server. Mark's Claude desktop app connects to it with a personal token. He opens Claude and says *"I want to write an article about navicular syndrome, here are my notes"*. He reads what comes back. He says *"that's too technical"*, or *"add a section on shoeing"*, as many times as he likes. Then he says *"push that to Hoofflix as a draft"*, and it appears in the admin panel for review.

On top of that he has three skills that switch themselves on without him doing anything: one that writes articles in the house style, one that builds an entire course — modules, lessons, quizzes, interactive activities — from a book chapter at whatever level he names, from Diploma up to Fellowship, and one that finds and verifies real Harvard-style references. He attaches a chapter, says *"do a Level 3 and a Level 5 version of this"*, and gets two complete courses.

No terminal. No admin login. No file paths. He talks, and it lands.

The thing that made it work wasn't the prompting. It was where I put the safety. **Every tool on that connector can only ever create a draft.** Nothing goes live without a human pressing Publish. The platform independently validates the shape of everything arriving and throws out anything malformed. The guardrails live in the system, not in the instructions — which means Mark genuinely cannot break anything, and I could tell him so honestly rather than just reassuringly.

That distinction is the whole thing. I didn't teach him to be careful. I built something where carelessness is harmless, and then told him the truth about it. Once he believed it — and believing it took a few weeks and a couple of deliberate "go on, try to break it" sessions — the fear went, and what was left was a man who is very good at his subject talking about his subject.

He now produces more in a week than we used to get in a month, in his own voice, properly referenced. He asks for features. He argues with me about the maths in the laminitis engine and he's usually right. Somewhere in there the dynamic stopped being developer-and-client.

I'd gently suggest that's the actual story about AI adoption, and it's not the one usually told. The barrier is almost never capability. It's that people are frightened of breaking something expensive in front of someone who'll judge them for it. Remove both halves of that and the "technophobe" turns out to have been a perfectly capable person in a badly designed situation.

## Where It's Actually At

I'm not going to pretend it's finished, because it isn't. Hoof Analysis is built and sitting behind a launch flag while it's verified. HoofScan is in TestFlight. The Pro Plus auto-site engine is designed and not yet built. The migration of 143 legacy members off the old WordPress subscriptions is being done one at a time, carefully, because they're real people's real direct debits.

But the shape is right, and the shape was the hard part. When it's done it will be, without much argument, the most complete system the equine professional world has — and not because of any single clever feature. It's because somebody finally sat down and asked what a farrier's whole week looks like, rather than just what a lesson looks like.

## What I'd Take From This

**Tell them the truth while it still costs you something.** The fortnight-in conversation where I said the marketing wasn't the problem is the reason there's a platform at all. If you're being paid for the wrong work, saying so is the most valuable thing you can do, and it's the thing that buys you every conversation afterwards.

**The retention feature is rarely the headline feature.** People join Hoofflix for the video library. They stay for the CPD report and the handout generator. Work out what makes your customer's actual week easier, and build that.

**AI tooling is only as good as the safety net under it.** A nearly-70-year-old technophobe runs agentic workflows here every day, and not because he became technical. It's because the system was built so nothing he does can hurt it.

**And the one underneath all of them:** the hardest part of a project like this was never the 235 database tables. It was the two years of a subject expert deciding, slowly, that his life's work was safe with me. You can't schedule that and you can't charge for it. You can only keep turning up and being straight with people.
