Most trade buyers have made their minds up about you before they pick up the phone. They've been on your website, looked at what you make, checked whether it fits their machine or their job, and compared you with two or three others. If your site couldn't answer their questions, you don't get a bad phone call. You just don't get a call.

That isn't guesswork. In the [2026 State of Marketing to Engineers report](https://advertising.globalspec.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/SMTE-Report_GS2602_FINAL.pdf) from GlobalSpec and TREW Marketing, a survey of over a thousand engineers and technical buyers worldwide, respondents said that on average 62% of the buying process happens online before they speak to anyone at the supplier. 74% said supplier websites are somewhere they routinely go when researching a purchase. [Gartner's research](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2020-09-15-gartner-says-80--of-b2b-sales-interactions-between-su) points the same way across B2B buying generally: buyers spend only 17% of their time meeting potential suppliers when they're considering a purchase.

So your website is doing the first part of the sales conversation without you. Here's what buyers are actually checking while it does.

I run a production business myself, so I know quotes, lead times and job sheets from the inside. I also build websites and software for [engineering and manufacturing firms](/engineering). Harford Attachments and J Taylor Horseboxes are two of the manufacturers whose websites I look after. This list comes from both sides of the counter.

## 1. Can I Tell in Seconds What You Make?

The homepage has one job before anyone scrolls: what do you make, who is it for, and can you make it for me?

A lot of manufacturer sites open with a slogan. "Precision engineering solutions." "Quality you can trust." Neither tells a buyer anything. Compare that with a headline like "Laser cutting, folding and fabrication in mild steel and stainless, from one-offs to batches of thousands." That's an example I've made up, but you can see the difference. A buyer knows in five seconds whether to keep reading.

Then make the navigation match how buyers think. If they search by machine (a 13 tonne excavator, a particular tractor), let them browse by machine. If they think in industries (food processing, agriculture, quarrying), give them that route in. If they think in processes (machining, fabrication, finishing), use those. Your internal product codes are for you, not them.

## 2. Specs, Dimensions and Compatibility

Engineers and buyers need numbers. Dimensions, weights, materials, tolerances, load ratings, finishes. And very often the whole question is: will it fit? Which machines, which couplers, which mounting pattern, which voltage.

In the same report, 34% of technical buyers said their most recent significant purchase was decided entirely on technical specs, pricing or performance, regardless of brand. For that buyer, a missing spec isn't a small gap. It's a reason to pick someone else.

A few things that make a real difference:

**Put the key specs on the page as text.** Not only inside a PDF. Text loads instantly on a phone, Google can read it, and the buyer doesn't have to download a file to check one dimension.

**Offer the data sheet as a download as well.** Buyers attach them to internal requests and supplier approvals. Give each file a sensible name ("XB450-data-sheet.pdf", not "Document1_final.pdf").

**Offer CAD files if you make components.** If an engineer can drop your part straight into their model, you're halfway onto the drawing.

**Keep each spec in one place.** If the website, the PDF and the printed brochure disagree, the buyer trusts none of them. A proper [product catalogue](/engineering/product-catalogues) stores each spec once and feeds both the web page and the data sheet from the same data.

That's why the website I built for [Harford Attachments](https://www.harfordattachments.co.uk), who make excavator attachments, has a proper product catalogue rather than a handful of brochure pages. Buyers of technical products shop by product.

## 3. Accreditations, Capability and Capacity

Before a buyer adds you to an approved supplier list, someone usually has to tick some boxes. Make those boxes easy to tick.

**Accreditations.** ISO 9001, ISO 14001, ISO 45001, EN 1090 if you fabricate structural steelwork, and any welding or industry approvals you hold. Give them their own page with the certificate number and the certifying body, and let people download the certificate. A strip of logos in the footer isn't much use to someone filling in a supplier questionnaire.

**Capability.** Which processes do you do in house, and which do you subcontract? Which materials do you work with? What's the biggest and smallest thing you can make?

**Capacity.** A machine list is one of the most useful pages a manufacturer can publish. Bed sizes, press brake tonnage and length, maximum turning diameter, number of axes. Engineers read it the way you'd read the spec sheet of a van before buying one. It tells them in a minute whether you can make their part.

## 4. Lead Times

This is the question buyers most want answered and suppliers least like answering in public. I understand why. Lead times move, and nobody wants to be held to last month's figure.

But the same research found that pricing and stock questions were the most common reason technical buyers first contacted a supplier (26%), followed by wanting to check what they'd found online (23%). If your site gives a rough answer, the calls you get are from people who already know you're in the right ballpark.

You don't have to promise a date. "Stock items usually dispatched within two working days, made-to-order typically 3 to 5 weeks, ring for current lead times" is honest and useful. Just make sure someone updates it when things change. A lead time that's obviously stale is worse than none.

## 5. Proof: Real Work and Real Sectors

Buyers want to see that you've done this before, for someone like them.

Photos of real parts and real jobs beat stock images every time. Nobody believes a photo of a handshake. A short project write-up (what the customer needed, what you made, any awkward constraints) does more than a page of adjectives. If you can name customers, do. If you can't, name the sector: "brackets for a food processing line", "replacement wear parts for quarry plant".

A clear list of the sectors you serve helps too. A buyer can tell in seconds whether you're likely to understand their world.

## 6. A Clear Way to Get a Quote, and a Form That Asks the Right Questions

Every product page should have an obvious "request a quote" button. Not buried at the bottom of the contact page.

Then the form itself. Most quote forms ask for a name, an email and a message, which means your first reply is a list of questions. That's a day lost on both sides. A good quote form asks for what you actually need to price the job:

- What they need (product, part number or a description)
- Quantity, and whether it's a one-off or a repeat order
- Material and finish, if relevant
- Machine make and model, for anything that has to fit
- Drawings or photos (accept PDF, DXF and STEP files, and phone photos)
- When they need it, and the delivery postcode
- Name, company, phone and email

Make the essentials required and the rest optional, so it doesn't feel like a tax return. Then tell people what happens next. "We reply to quote requests within one working day" sets expectations and makes you look organised.

If you want to go further, a [quote tool or configurator](/engineering/quote-tools) lets buyers pick options and see a price or send a fully specified request. And if your quotes currently come out of a spreadsheet, I've written about [moving quoting off spreadsheets](/blog/replacing-spreadsheet-quoting) without the pain.

## 7. A Phone Number That Works on a Phone

A lot of trade buyers are on site, in a yard or in a cab when they look you up. They want to tap your number and ring you.

So put the number at the top of every page, make it a proper tap-to-call link, and don't put it inside an image where a phone can't do anything with it. Show your opening hours. If a mobile or WhatsApp number gets a quicker answer, say so. And check it goes to someone who'll pick up, not a switchboard that rings out.

## 8. Speed on a Phone With One Bar of Signal

Your website might load nicely on the office PC. Now try it on a phone, outside, at the far end of your own yard.

That's how a lot of your buyers see it. It matters for search as well, because [Google uses the mobile version of your site](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/mobile/mobile-sites-mobile-first-indexing) for indexing and ranking.

The usual culprits on manufacturer sites:

- Photos straight off a camera, several megabytes each
- Full-screen video or image sliders on the homepage
- Product information only available as large PDFs
- Old page builders and plugins loading code the page never uses

None of these are hard to fix. Resize the images, drop the slider, put the specs in the page, and the site gets noticeably quicker.

## A Five-Minute Self-Check

Open your site on your phone and ask yourself:

1. Could a stranger tell what we make, and for whom, without scrolling?
2. Can they find a product by machine, industry or process, the way they'd think of it?
3. Are the key specs on the page, with a data sheet to download?
4. Can they find our accreditations and certificate numbers?
5. Is there any indication of lead times?
6. Is there real proof: photos, projects, sectors?
7. Is "request a quote" obvious, and does the form ask for what we need?
8. Can they tap the phone number and get through to a person?
9. Did all of that load quickly on 4G?

If you answered no to more than two or three, your website is probably losing you enquiries you'll never know about.
